Credits are the currency used across ArcGIS. They are spent on two different things, and conflating them is where most credit anxiety comes from. Storage is a recurring monthly rent on everything you host. Transactions are one-time charges for geocoding, analysis, routing, printing and premium content. This tool models both against the credits your organization actually has.

The published Esri rates are fixed and are used as-is. The part that needs judgment is how large your hosted data will be once it lands in ArcGIS Online. That part is an engineering estimate, not an Esri figure, so section 02 includes a script that measures your real geometry in ArcGIS Pro and loads it straight into the table. Nothing is sent anywhere; everything runs in your browser.

Use it in three passes

  • Set your credit supply from your user type mix or your real balance.
  • List what you intend to host. Use the presets as starting points and correct them.
  • Measure your real geometry in ArcGIS Pro and load it in, then re-read the charts.

01 Your credit supply

Every subscription includes a pool of credits shared by the organization, sized by the user types you hold. Creator, Professional and Professional Plus include 500 each; Mobile Worker and Contributor include 250. Viewer and Editor include none.

Unused subscription credits expire at the end of the subscription term, so the planning unit is the subscription year, not the life of the project. Credits you purchase separately expire after two years.

If you already know your balance, skip the user type math and enter it directly. An organization that spends its entire balance enters a restricted state: existing content keeps working, but no one can publish anything new.

02 What you are hosting

One row per hosted item. Feature layers are sized from geometry and attributes, because vertex count is the dominant term in a hosted feature layer and attribute width is second. Imagery, tile caches, packages, documents and attachments are billed on their file size instead, so enter those directly in GB.

What the modeled size is built from. Esri publishes credits per megabyte, not megabytes per feature, so the size column is an engineering estimate rather than a published figure: 16 bytes per vertex (two 8-byte doubles), 24 bytes per attribute value, 48 bytes of row overhead, then 1.60x for indexes and storage structures and a further 1.70x where sync is on. Those are reasonable working numbers, not an authority. Use Scope data above to fix the vertex and field counts from your own geometry, and check one published layer against your organization status dashboard before a number here goes into a budget, a proposal or a client conversation.

If you moved to a Premium feature data store

Reading the Premium comparison. The prices above are Esri list prices as of September 2026: M2 $2,800 per month with 2 TB of feature data storage, M3 $4,950 with 3 TB, M4 $9,500 with 4 TB. They are list, not your price, so overwrite them with what your agreement or quote actually says before the numbers leave this page. Every field recalculates as you type. M2 is the entry level, each level doubles the database processing power of the one below it, and M4 is sixteen times the compute of a Standard feature data store. There are two separate decisions here. Capacity and performance pick the level; the break-even decides whether Premium beats credits at all. A Standard feature data store tops out at 500 GB of feature data, so past that Premium is required rather than optional. Below it, Premium removes feature storage from the credit model without removing the cost, and is worth it when feature storage dominates your burn, when heavy query, edit or ETL loads need dedicated database resources, or when predictable billing matters more than a lower absolute number.

05 What to do about it

Section 04 says what the plan costs. This one says what to buy. There are only two levers: purchase more credits, or move feature storage out of the credit model onto a Premium feature data store and pay a fixed subscription instead. Both are priced below against the same scenario, so the comparison is like for like.

Credits are sold in blocks of 1,000 and you cannot buy a partial block, so the figures below round up to whole blocks. Each option covers only the shortfall that its own configuration leaves behind: moving to Premium removes feature storage from the burn first, then buys credits for whatever is still uncovered.

Recommendation

Cheapest option that actually holds your data.

What this does not decide for you. It is a cost comparison, and cost is not the only reason to move. Premium buys dedicated database compute, which is the right call when heavy query, edit or ETL loads are the problem even if credits would be cheaper. It also buys predictable billing, which some budgets value more than a lower number. Going the other way, purchased credits expire two years after purchase and unused subscription credits expire at the end of each term, so buying several years of headroom in one go can cost more than it saves. Treat the recommendation as the budget answer and make the performance call separately.

Four practical levers, in the order they usually pay off. Generalize the handful of dense shapes carrying your storage before you touch anything else. Trim fields you do not publish, since every field is paid for in every row, every month. Turn on Enable Sync and change tracking only where offline or editing is a real requirement, because both grow the stored size over time. And delete or archive superseded content, since storage is charged hourly and stops the moment the item is gone.

Scope your data

Measure real geometry in ArcGIS Pro, then load it straight into the table. Each line you paste back becomes a row with your own feature count, mean vertex count and field count.

Step 1 · run in the ArcGIS Pro Python window 
# Measure feature classes for the AGOL Credit Planner.
# Copy the PLANNER lines from the output and paste them
# into the planner, section 02.
import arcpy, statistics

FCS = [
    r"C:\data\Base.gdb\Parcels",
    # add more feature classes here
]

for fc in FCS:
    name = arcpy.Describe(fc).baseName
    v = [r[0].pointCount for r
         in arcpy.da.SearchCursor(fc, ["SHAPE@"])
         if r[0]]
    f = [x for x in arcpy.ListFields(fc)
         if x.type not in ("OID", "Geometry")
         and x.name.lower() not in
         ("shape_length", "shape_area")]
    if not v:
        print(name, "has no geometry, skipped")
        continue
    print("{}  features {:,}  mean {:.1f}  "
          "median {:.0f}  max {:,}  fields {}".format(
          name, len(v), statistics.mean(v),
          statistics.median(v), max(v), len(f)))
    print("PLANNER|{}|{}|{:.1f}|{}".format(
          name, len(v), statistics.mean(v), len(f)))

The readable line is for you: a layer whose mean sits far above its median has a handful of very dense shapes carrying the storage, and generalizing just those is usually the cheapest optimization available. The PLANNER line is for the box below.

Step 2 · paste the PLANNER lines back

Lines are PLANNER|name|features|mean vertices|attribute fields. Paste as many as you measured; the script prints one per feature class. Rows land in the table above as Feature type with growth and sync off, so set those yourself for anything being actively collected.

Measuring replaces the two inputs the presets are most often wrong about. It does not verify the byte assumptions listed below, so after you publish one of these layers, compare its real size on the organization status dashboard against the modeled size in the table. If they are far apart, the assumptions need revisiting, not your measurements.
